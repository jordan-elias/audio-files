On sound that thinks,
and networks that listen

How it works

Neural Synthesis models a network of twelve nodes connected by weighted, directed edges. When the network runs, three simultaneous processes unfold at different speeds: signal propagation at fifty milliseconds (the fast layer, governing immediate audio response), Hebbian weight evolution at two to thirty seconds (the medium layer, governing the emergence of texture and harmonic coherence), and structural reorganization over minutes (the slow layer, where quiet connections are pruned and active nodes spontaneously form new ones).

None of the sound is pre-composed. Every texture, every rhythmic pattern, every harmonic consonance that emerges does so through the interaction of the network with itself — through feedback, through learning, through the physics of oscillating systems that want to synchronize.

To listen: Start the network, set instability to 40–60%, and wait. The first thirty seconds are unresolved. After two to three minutes, connections begin to strengthen, delay nodes phase-lock to the tempo, and you will hear something that sounds almost intentional. It was not. It grew.
Node types and signal routing

The network contains four kinds of nodes, each contributing differently to the sound. Oscillator nodes self-generate. They produce a continuous waveform — sine, triangle, or a cluster of slightly detuned partials — whose frequency drifts slowly toward the nearest harmonic partial of the network's root pitch. Their output flows along outgoing edges to neighboring nodes.

Filter nodes do not self-generate. They receive incoming signals, apply a low-pass or bandpass filter whose cutoff frequency is driven by the strength of the incoming signal, and pass the shaped audio onward. A filter node in a strong feedback loop develops a slowly sweeping resonance — not because anything programmed it to, but because the signal driving its cutoff is itself evolving.

Nonlinear nodes apply activation functions — tanh, wave-folding, sigmoid, and others — to their input. Two pure sine waves entering a tanh function produce a spectrum containing harmonics that neither wave contained alone. These nodes are the harmonic engine of the network: complexity emerges not from complexity of input, but from nonlinear transformation of simple signals.

Delay nodes hold their input in a short ring buffer and replay it with a time offset between ten milliseconds and eight hundred milliseconds. When rhythmic gravity is enabled, these delay times are slowly attracted toward sub-multiples of the global tempo period — quarter notes, triplets, eighth notes — creating rhythmic structure that emerges organically from the network's own activity.

The three timescales

Real neural systems — and real music — operate across multiple timescales simultaneously. A single neuron fires in milliseconds. A synaptic connection strengthens over minutes. The architecture of a brain region takes years to develop. Neural Synthesis implements three analogous timescales, each producing a different quality of evolution.

The fast layer (fifty milliseconds) governs immediate signal propagation: energy injected into one node travels to its neighbors within one tick. This is what you hear as direct sonic response — pressing a key and hearing a sound. It is also where the physics of oscillation operates: phase relationships, interference patterns, and instantaneous amplitude.

The medium layer (two to thirty seconds) is where texture emerges. Hebbian learning gradually strengthens connections between co-active nodes. Oscillator frequencies drift toward consonant intervals with their most-connected neighbors. Delay nodes begin locking to tempo sub-multiples. Filter cutoffs shift as the signals driving them evolve. This is the layer most audible as "the piece changing" — the sense that what you're hearing now was not what you heard two minutes ago, and that the difference feels intentional even though it wasn't.

The slow layer (five to sixty minutes) is structural. Connections whose weights have remained near zero for many ticks are pruned — the network becomes sparser, more specialized. New connections spontaneously form between nodes that have been consistently co-active. Nodes that have been continuously silent lower their activation threshold until they participate (intrinsic plasticity). This is the timescale of the piece developing a character — of something that, if you listen over an hour, will sound genuinely different at the end than at the beginning, not because of random drift, but because it has learned something from its own activity.

Hebbian learning

Hebbian learning is one of the oldest and most durable ideas in neuroscience. Donald Hebb proposed it in 1949, before anyone had the tools to verify it directly: the strength of a connection between two neurons increases when both neurons are active at the same time. Neurons that fire together wire together. Connections that are never simultaneously active weaken and fade.

In Neural Synthesis, every edge has a weight that evolves according to this rule. When node A is active and node B is active and A connects to B, that connection strengthens. Over many ticks, the network's weight matrix sculpts itself into a map of its own history — which nodes have been co-active, how often, how strongly. This is not optimization toward a goal. There is no target output, no error signal, no correct answer. The network learns only what it has done.

The consequence, musically, is that the network develops tendencies. A path that has been active many times becomes preferred — signals flow more easily through it, shaping which nodes influence which others. The network begins to have a characteristic sound, a set of preferred resonances, a rhythm of activity that it has generated for itself from nothing but the Hebbian rule and whatever initial conditions you set.

Weight sign matters: Positive weights are excitatory — they amplify their target. Negative weights are inhibitory — they suppress. The weight matrix display shows this: green for excitatory, orange for inhibitory. Watch it evolve as the network runs. Some connections you expect to strengthen will weaken; some you don't expect will form.
Phase coupling and the Kuramoto model

The most audibly striking phenomenon in Neural Synthesis is phase lock: the moment when two oscillators, previously beating against each other in an unstable interference pattern, suddenly synchronize — their phases align, their signals reinforce, and a coherent tone emerges from what was dissonance. The emergence annotation layer marks these moments. You will hear them before you read them.

Phase locking is governed by a simplified version of the Kuramoto model. Each oscillator has a phase (where it currently is in its cycle) and a natural frequency. When two oscillators are strongly connected (high Hebbian weight), they exert a phase-attracting force on each other proportional to the sine of their phase difference. If the coupling is strong enough and their frequencies are close enough, they lock. If the coupling is weak, they continue to beat.

The phase coupling parameter controls the strength of this attraction. At low values, oscillators evolve independently — many simultaneous beating patterns, a complex, restless texture. At high values, clusters of oscillators lock together — cleaner harmonics, more stable but less alive. The interesting range is in between: some nodes locked, others not, with occasional phase-slip events where a locked pair decouples as their weights shift.

This mechanism interacts with Hebbian learning in a crucial way: oscillators that phase-lock become more consistently co-active, which strengthens their Hebbian connection, which increases their phase coupling, which makes the lock more stable. It is a positive feedback loop that produces long-term coherence from short-term coincidence. The network doesn't decide to develop a stable harmonic relationship. It falls into one, and then reinforces it by having fallen there.

Synaptic depression and homeostasis

Two biological mechanisms prevent Neural Synthesis from getting stuck in a single loud feedback loop. Both are directly derived from the physiology of real synapses.

Synaptic depression reduces the gain of a connection that has been continuously active. A loop that has been loud for several seconds will begin to quiet — not because the weight has changed (Hebbian learning operates on a slower timescale) but because the synapse is temporarily depleted. Recovery takes roughly ten seconds. The effect is that even a very strong feedback loop will pulse rhythmically rather than sustain indefinitely — energy accumulates, peaks, depletes, recovers, accumulates again. This is the origin of the slow rhythmic gating you will hear in the medium timescale: a sound that emerges, builds, then fades, then returns. It was not composed. It is the physics of the synapse.

Intrinsic plasticity (homeostasis) operates at the node level. A node that has been consistently quiet gradually lowers its activation threshold — becoming more sensitive, more likely to fire. A node that has been consistently overactive raises its threshold — becoming less responsive. Over many minutes, this tends the network toward balanced activity: no node permanently silent, no node permanently saturating. The network maintains itself in a productive state, near the edge of disorder but not in it — what neuroscientists call the critical regime.

Harmonic coherence and the tuning system

Neural Synthesis uses a tuning system based on just intonation rather than equal temperament. Each oscillator is assigned a harmonic role — a partial in the overtone series — and its frequency is weakly attracted toward its just-tuned target. The attraction is not strong enough to enforce tuning; nodes that are isolated or frequently active will drift away. But nodes that are strongly Hebbianly connected tend to drift toward each other, and if those drifts happen to be toward consonant intervals, the phase coupling reinforces them.

The result is that the network gradually develops a harmonic field — not a key in the Western sense, but a set of preferred frequency relationships that have emerged from the network's own learning. Early in a session, you hear beating and dissonance as nodes with arbitrarily assigned frequencies interact. Over twenty to thirty minutes, the beating slowly resolves — not completely, not perfectly, but perceptibly — into something that has the quality of a mode or a tuning. Different sessions, with different topologies and initial conditions, converge to different tunings. The network composes its own scale.

The spatial canvas and anchor mode

In default mode, the canvas uses a force-directed layout: nodes repel each other and are attracted toward their connected neighbors, settling into a natural arrangement that visually reflects the network's topology. In anchor mode (press the anchor button), the force simulation pauses. You can drag nodes to intentional positions. Proximity now has physical meaning: connection strength between two nodes is modulated by their distance — closer nodes interact more strongly, even if their Hebbian weight is low.

This turns the canvas into a compositional surface. Grouping oscillator nodes tightly together creates strong mutual coupling — they will tend to phase-lock and develop shared rhythms. Placing a filter node between two oscillator clusters puts it in the signal path between them, shaping what flows from one cluster to the other. Positioning delay nodes at the periphery creates echo chambers that receive signals from the main activity and return them with time offsets.

The spatial arrangement is also directly audible through stereo panning: a node's horizontal position on the canvas maps to its position in the stereo field. Moving nodes is moving sound sources in an acoustic space. The room you build is the room you hear.

David Tudor and feedback instruments

David Tudor spent the 1960s and 1970s building electronic instruments from scratch — not instruments in any traditional sense, but networks of circuits, each feeding signal into the next, listening and responding in ways no single hand could predict or control. Rainforest (1968) distributed transducers across physical objects — metal sheets, ceramic bowls, wooden planks, springs — letting each surface shape and color sound according to its own resonant character. No two performances were identical. No single performance could be replicated. The piece was the network, and the network was always changing.

Tudor's key insight was the role of the performer in a feedback system. When a circuit's output becomes its input, the performer cannot specify what will happen — they can only set the conditions under which something happens. The role shifts from expression to cultivation: establishing the ecological conditions in which interesting behavior can emerge, then listening rather than directing. Tudor would spend hours adjusting a single parameter, not to achieve a target sound but to find the boundary where the system became interesting — unstable enough to be surprising, stable enough to be coherent.

The instability parameter in Neural Synthesis is this boundary in numerical form. Below 30%, the network is too stable — it settles into a drone and stays there. Above 70%, it is too chaotic — signals accumulate too fast, nonlinear nodes saturate, and the result is noise rather than texture. The interesting range is the middle: where feedback accumulates but doesn't flood, where signals reinforce but don't overwhelm, where structure and surprise coexist. Tudor found this range by ear over years of practice. You can find it in minutes. The search is the same.

Neural networks as instruments

A neural network, in its most abstract form, is a directed graph: a set of nodes and the weighted connections between them. Each node receives signals from its incoming connections, combines them (usually as a weighted sum), and applies a nonlinear transformation to produce an output that flows to outgoing connections. The weights determine what the network does. Learning — in machine learning, through backpropagation; in Neural Synthesis, through Hebbian learning — changes the weights over time.

The distinction between a trained neural network and Neural Synthesis is the distinction between optimization and emergence. A trained network has a target: minimize the error on a specific task. Every weight update is calculated to move the network toward that target. The result is a network that is very good at one thing and has no independent existence — it is a function, not a system.

Neural Synthesis has no target, no error signal, no optimization. Weights evolve according to local rules (Hebbian learning) without any global objective. The network does not become "better" at anything. It becomes something. Over time, it develops tendencies, preferences, characteristic behaviors — not because these were designed, but because they emerged from the interaction of the Hebbian rule with the particular initial conditions of this session. This is emergence in the technical sense: global behavior arising from local rules, where the global behavior is not specified by and cannot be derived from the local rules alone.

The brain

The brain is, at its substrate, a network: roughly 86 billion neurons, each connected on average to 7,000 others, exchanging electrochemical signals across synapses whose strength changes with every firing. What makes it remarkable is not the complexity of any individual neuron — a single cell is relatively simple, following approximately the same rules as a node in Neural Synthesis — but the patterns of coordinated activity that emerge across billions of them simultaneously. Consciousness, perception, memory: none of these live in a single place. They are processes, distributed across the network's behavior in time.

One of the most persistent features of brain activity is oscillation. Neurons across distant regions synchronize their firing into rhythmic waves — alpha, beta, theta, gamma — at frequencies that shift with attention, sleep, emotion, and cognitive load. These oscillations are not noise. They are the brain's coordination mechanism. When two regions need to communicate, they synchronize. When they need to decouple, their oscillations drift apart. The Kuramoto model of coupled oscillators, which governs phase locking in Neural Synthesis, was developed partly as a model of how brain regions synchronize.

Emergence is what happens when local interactions produce global behavior that no individual element contains or could predict. A single neuron does not hear; billions of them, organized into the right patterns of connectivity and timing, produce auditory experience. In Neural Synthesis, individual oscillator nodes are trivial — a sine wave at a harmonic frequency. But as connections form, weights evolve, feedback propagates through delay and nonlinear transformation, and phase coupling pulls oscillators into alignment, textures arise that none of the nodes produce alone. The network begins to have a character — a way of being — that emerges from the interaction of a small set of rules applied consistently over time. This is not a metaphor for neural emergence. It is the same phenomenon at a different scale: local rules, global behavior, and the irreducible surprise of a system that is genuinely listening to itself.

When you sit with Neural Synthesis for an hour, adjusting nothing, simply listening as it evolves, you are attending to a process that shares its fundamental logic with the process attending to it. The network learns from its own activity. So do you.
